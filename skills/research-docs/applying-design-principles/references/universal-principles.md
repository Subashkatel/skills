# Universal Principles of Design: Synthesis for Technical Slides and Figures

Source: Lidwell, Holden, Butler, "Universal Principles of Design" (extracted text). This retained synthesis contains reported numerical guidance and interpretation. The original book is absent from the indexed local collection, so exact quotations and page locators require checking an available edition. Numbers are heuristics, not universal thresholds or accessibility compliance tests. Framing throughout: research talks, one idea per slide, minimal style.

## 1. Layout and space

### Horror Vacui
(a) The tendency to fill blank spaces with objects and elements rather than leave them empty; in style, "the opposite of minimalism."
(b) The book reports "a general inverse relationship between horror vacui and value perception: as horror vacui increases, perceived value decreases." Evidence: a survey of more than 100 clothing stores found the degree to which shop windows were filled was inversely related to average price and brand prestige; high-end boutiques often used "a single mannequin, no hanging or stacked clothes, no signage, and no price tags." The relationship may track audience affluence or education: "for those accustomed to having more, less is more." Prescription: favor minimalism for affluent and well-educated audiences; for information-rich media, use alignment and chunking to keep dense pages organized while mitigating horror vacui.
(c) For slides this means: an expert research audience reads empty space as confidence and value; a filled slide reads as a bulk-sales shop window.

### Gutenberg Diagram
(a) Western readers scan evenly distributed, homogeneous information from a primary optical area (top left) in left-to-right sweeps down to a terminal area (bottom right), driven by "reading gravity."
(b) Four quadrants: primary optical (top left), strong fallow (top right), weak fallow (bottom left), terminal (bottom right); fallow areas "receive minimal attention unless visually emphasized." Key caveat the book stresses: the diagram is "likely only predictive of eye movement for heavy text information, evenly distributed and homogeneous information, and blank pages"; in all other cases "the weight of the elements ... will direct eye movements" (a heavy central headline makes the center the primary optical area). There is little empirical evidence it improves reading rate or comprehension.
(c) For slides this means: on a text-heavy slide put the claim top left and the takeaway or citation bottom right; on a slide dominated by one figure, the figure's visual weight, not the diagram, steers the eye, so place it wherever you want attention.

### Rule of Thirds
(a) Divide the medium into thirds vertically and horizontally, creating nine rectangles and four intersections, and place the primary element on an intersection.
(b) The 2/3 section gives ratio 0.666 versus the golden ratio 0.618, a rough but easy approximation. When the primary element is so strong it imbalances the composition, center it instead, especially when surrounding space reinforces it. If not reinforced, add a counterpoint element at the opposing intersection. Align a strong vertical or horizontal element along a grid line of matching orientation.
(c) For slides this means: put the key plot or headline number on a thirds intersection for an interesting asymmetric layout, or dead center when it is the lone strong element on the slide.

### Golden Ratio
(a) A height-to-width or segment ratio approximating 0.618 (bc/ab = ab/ac = 0.618; adding 1 yields Phi, 1.618).
(b) A substantial body of research supports a preference for golden-ratio rectangles, though the findings have been challenged (experimenter bias, methodological flaws). Prescription: "Geometries of a design should not be contrived to create golden ratios, but golden ratios should be explored when other aspects of the design are not compromised."
(c) For slides this means: golden proportions are a reasonable default for figure aspect ratios and text-block placement, but never distort content to hit the ratio.

### Alignment
(a) Elements should line up along common rows, columns, or centerlines; alignment creates unity, cohesion, and perceived stability, and leads the eye through a design.
(b) Left-aligned and right-aligned text blocks give stronger alignment cues than center-aligned blocks (the invisible column is a clear visual cue); centered blocks are "visually ambiguous" and hard to connect to other elements. Justified text provides more alignment cues than unjustified and suits complex compositions with many elements. For diagonal alignments, relative angles between alignment paths should be 30 degrees or greater; less than 30 degrees "is too subtle and difficult to detect." Edges of the medium and centerlines count as alignment elements. Misalignment for attention or tension is a rare, deliberate exception.
(c) For slides this means: hang every title, label, and figure on a shared grid; prefer left-aligned text over centered, and treat any two elements off-grid as a bug unless the misalignment is the point.

### Area Alignment
(a) Align nonuniform, asymmetric elements by their visual area or weight, not by their edges; software edge-alignment "is one of the most common errors in graphic design."
(b) Position the object so equal visual weight hangs on either side of the alignment axis, as if balanced on it; this creates a ragged edge and requires letting parts of elements hang into margins next to rectilinear elements. Pull quotes should align on the text edge with quotation marks hanging; numbered or bulleted items should align on the text edge with numbers and bullets hanging, unless items are meant to be subordinate. Simple symmetric objects: edge-align. Dissimilar shapes: area-align by eye.
(c) For slides this means: optically center logos, equations, and irregular figures rather than trusting the software's bounding-box center, and hang bullets and quote marks outside the text column.

### Symmetry
(a) Visual equivalence among elements in a form; long associated with beauty, in three types: reflection, rotation, and translation.
(b) Symmetric forms tend to be seen as figure rather than ground, so they get more attention and are better recalled; they are simpler than asymmetric forms, aiding recognition and recall. Use symmetry to convey balance, harmony, and stability; use simple symmetric forms when recognition and recall matter, and combinations of symmetry types when aesthetics and interestingness matter.
(c) For slides this means: symmetric layouts for architecture diagrams you want remembered; asymmetric (thirds-based) layouts when you want visual interest.

### Entry Point
(a) The point of physical or attentional entry into a design; first impressions formed there govern subsequent perceptions and interactions.
(b) Three elements of good entry-point design: minimal barriers (nothing impeding entry, including "noisy displays with many unnecessary elements" and aesthetic barriers), points of prospect (let people orient and survey options without feeling hurried), and progressive lures (elements that pull people incrementally deeper, like headlines or page references).
(c) For slides this means: each slide needs one obvious place to start looking, no clutter blocking it, and a cue (title, arrow, emphasis) that lures the eye onward into the evidence.

### Figure-Ground Relationship
(a) Perception separates everything into figure (object of focus) or ground (undifferentiated background); when the relationship is clear it is stable.
(b) "The figure element receives more attention and is better remembered than the ground." Cues that make something a figure: definite shape, ground continuing behind it, seeming closer with a clear location, being below a horizon line, and being in the lower regions of a design (elements in upper regions read as ground). Ambiguous figure-ground alternates and confuses.
(c) For slides this means: make the data trace or claim the unambiguous figure by giving it shape, contrast, and clear location, and keep backgrounds, watermarks, and gridlines recessive so they never compete for figure status.

## 2. Reducing clutter vs filling space

### Signal-to-Noise Ratio
(a) The ratio of relevant to irrelevant information in a display; "the highest possible signal-to-noise ratio is desirable in design."
(b) Maximize signal: simple, concise presentation; the correct graph type for the data; emphasize key aspects via highlighting or redundant coding. Minimize noise: "every unnecessary data item, graphic, line, or symbol steals attention away from relevant elements"; remove unnecessary elements, and minimize the expression of necessary ones, for example lines in grids and tables "should be thinned, lightened, and possibly even removed." "Every element in a design should be expressed to the extent necessary, but not beyond the extent necessary. Excess is noise."
(c) For slides this means: delete decorative elements outright and demote required scaffolding (axes, grids, boxes) to thin light gray so only the data carries visual weight.

### Ockham's Razor
(a) Given a choice between functionally equivalent designs, select the simplest.
(b) Unnecessary elements decrease efficiency and increase the probability of unanticipated consequences; "unnecessary weight, whether physical, visual, or cognitive, degrades performance." Procedure: among functionally equivalent designs pick the one with fewest visual elements, then remove as many elements as possible without compromising function, then minimize the expression of the remaining elements. The book cites Tufte: "Make all visual distinctions as subtle as possible, but still clear and effective."
(c) For slides this means: after drafting a slide, do two passes: remove elements, then mute what survives.

### Progressive Disclosure
(a) Display only necessary or requested information at any given time to prevent information overload.
(b) Separate information into layers and present only the relevant layer; information given before a person is ready to process it "is effectively noise"; gradual disclosure significantly reduces errors and recovery time. Especially valuable for novices and infrequent users. Footnoted caution: showing everything at once to save clicks "will significantly increase cognitive load."
(c) For slides this means: build one idea per slide, reveal details across successive slides or builds instead of preloading everything, and park secondary material in backup slides.

### Performance Load
(a) The greater the mental (cognitive) plus physical (kinematic) effort a task demands, the less likely it is accomplished successfully; load up means errors and time up.
(b) Reduce cognitive load by "eliminating unnecessary information from displays, chunking information that is to be remembered, providing memory aids," and automating memory- and computation-intensive tasks. Reduce kinematic load by cutting steps and motion.
(c) For slides this means: the audience's working memory is your bottleneck; every extra label, acronym, or cross-reference the viewer must hold or hunt for degrades comprehension of the actual result.

### Chunking
(a) Combine units of information into a limited number of chunks so information is easier to process and remember.
(b) "The maximum number of chunks that can be efficiently processed by short-term memory is four, plus or minus one." The book explicitly says to "use the contemporary estimate of 4 plus or minus 1 chunks," noting Miller's original 1956 estimate of 7 plus or minus 2 has been superseded (citing Cowan 2001). Example: most people can remember a list of five words for 30 seconds, few can remember ten. Critical scope limit: chunking applies only to tasks involving memory or problem solving. Chunking information that is meant to be searched or scanned is "a potential misapplication" that "would dramatically increase the scan time and effort, and yield no benefits." Chunk critical display information where noise or stress will degrade short-term memory.
(c) For slides this means: at most about four takeaways, curve labels, or grouped items the audience must carry forward; but a reference table meant for lookup need not be squeezed into four rows.

### Law of Prägnanz
(a) People interpret ambiguous images in the simplest way possible: fewer elements, symmetric rather than asymmetric arrangements.
(b) People visually process and remember simple figures better than complex figures, because complex images cost cognitive resources to re-encode as simpler forms. "Therefore, minimize the number of elements in a design." Symmetric compositions are perceived as simpler and more stable but less interesting: favor symmetry when efficiency of use is the priority, asymmetry when interestingness is the priority.
(c) For slides this means: diagrams built from few, regular, symmetric shapes are decoded and recalled with less effort; complexity in the drawing taxes the viewer even when the underlying idea is simple.

### Propositional Density
(a) The amount of information conveyed per unit element; PD = deep propositions (meanings) divided by surface propositions (perceptible elements).
(b) Designs with PD > 1 are perceived as more interesting and engaging than designs with PD < 1. "Simple objects and environments (few surface propositions) that are rich in meaning (many deep propositions) are perceived to be the most compelling." Examples: the Apple logo with PD = 2; the 2008 Obama logo with PD = 10/3 = 3.33. Deep propositions must be complementary; contradictory meanings confuse the message.
(c) For slides this means: the ideal slide element does double duty (a curve that is simultaneously the result and the method illustration); add meaning per element, not elements per slide.

## 3. Type and reading

### Legibility
(a) The visual clarity of text, based on size, typeface, contrast, text block, and spacing.
(b) The book's numbers: for printed text, "standard 9- to 12-point type is considered optimal"; smaller sizes only for captions and notes; "use larger type for low-resolution displays and more senior audiences." No performance difference between serif and sans serif: choose on aesthetics (but on low-resolution displays, for type smaller than 12 point, use sans serif without antialiasing). Use sentence case for text blocks. Contrast: "performance is optimal when contrast levels between text and background exceed 70 percent"; dark text on light background preferred; patterned or textured backgrounds "can dramatically reduce legibility, and should be avoided." Text blocks: for 9- to 12-point type, line length of 3 to 5 inches (8 to 13 cm), "a maximum of about 10 to 12 words per line, or 35 to 55 characters per line"; visual processing speed is greatest on long lines (80 characters or more) but readers prefer 35 to 55, so favor short lines unless speed is critical. No performance difference justified vs unjustified. Spacing: leading of type size plus 1 to 4 points; proportional over monospaced.
(c) For slides this means: high text-background contrast on a plain background, sentence case, lines held to roughly 35 to 55 characters, generous leading, and type scaled up well beyond print sizes since a projected slide is a low-resolution display read at distance.

### Readability
(a) The degree to which prose is understood, driven by word length, word commonality, sentence length, number of clauses, and syllables per sentence.
(b) "Complex information requires the simplest presentation possible." Omit needless words and punctuation without sacrificing clarity; avoid acronyms, jargon, and untranslated quotations; generally use active voice (passive when the message matters more than the messenger); match sentence length and grade level to the audience using readability formulas as a check, not a target.
(c) For slides this means: short plain sentences on slides, spelled-out terms on first use, and complexity carried by the figure, not the prose.

### Inverted Pyramid
(a) Present critical information first, then elaboration in descending order of importance (journalism's lead-then-body structure).
(b) The lead is a terse summary of what, where, when, who, why, how. Benefits listed: conveys key aspects quickly, establishes context for later facts, initial chunks are remembered better than later chunks, permits efficient scanning, and can be cut from the end. Cost: no suspense, can feel boring, so add engaging media or layout if interestingness suffers. Where the form cannot be inverted (standard scientific writing), give an executive summary up front.
(c) For slides this means: state the result in the slide title and lead slide; background, methods, and caveats come after the punchline, never before it.

### Serial Position Effects
(a) Items at the beginning (primacy) and end (recency) of a list are recalled better than items in the middle.
(b) Primacy comes from long-term memory storage and strengthens with slower presentation; recency comes from working memory, and "disappears when people think about other matters for thirty seconds" after the last item. For visual stimuli, early items have the greatest influence and color interpretation of later items; for auditory stimuli, late items dominate. If a decision follows immediately after presentation, the last position wins; otherwise the first. The illustration notes ballot order is "estimated to be worth between 1 percent and 4 percent of the vote."
(c) For slides this means: open and close the talk, and each section, with the claims you want remembered; anything buried mid-deck or mid-list needs extra emphasis to survive.

### von Restorff Effect
(a) Noticeably different things are more likely to be recalled than common things (difference in context or in experience attracts attention).
(b) Highlight key elements (e.g., bold text), but "if everything is highlighted, then nothing is highlighted, so apply the technique sparingly." Use distinctiveness to boost recall of items stuck in the weak middle of a list or sequence. Unusual words, constructions, and images are better remembered than typical ones.
(c) For slides this means: one visually distinct element per slide (the red curve among gray baselines) is a memory device; three distinct elements cancel each other.

### Highlighting
(a) A technique for bringing attention to an area of text or image; misapplied, it reduces performance.
(b) The book's specific rules: "Highlight no more than 10 percent of the visible design; highlighting effects are diluted as the percentage increases." Use a small number of techniques applied consistently. Bold is "generally preferred over other techniques as it adds minimal noise ... and clearly highlights target elements." Italics add minimal noise but are less detectable and legible. Underlining "adds considerable noise and compromises legibility, and should be used sparingly if at all." Uppercase is easily scanned for short labels and keywords in busy displays; avoid font changes as highlighting. Color: use sparingly, only together with other highlighting techniques, with "a few desaturated colors that are clearly distinct from one another." Inversing works for text but adds considerable noise. Blinking only for critical information demanding immediate response, and it must be dismissable.
(c) For slides this means: bold (or one reserved accent color) on at most a tenth of what is visible, applied the same way on every slide; never underline, never rely on color alone.

### Hierarchy
(a) Hierarchical organization is "the simplest structure for visualizing and understanding complexity"; making relationships visible increases knowledge of a system.
(b) Perception of hierarchy follows relative left-right and top-down position, plus proximity, size, and connecting lines. Three visual forms: trees (moderate complexity, overviews), nests (simple hierarchies and grouping, like Venn diagrams), stairs (complex hierarchies, outlines, but falsely imply sequence among siblings). Selectively reveal and conceal levels to maximize clarity.
(c) For slides this means: encode importance with position and size (big top-left title, subordinate detail lower and smaller), and pick tree, nest, or indented-outline form to match the complexity of what a diagram shows.

### Layering
(a) Organize information into related groupings and present only certain groupings at a time, to manage complexity and reinforce relationships.
(b) Two-dimensional layering shows one layer at a time: linear layers for stories and time-based sequences; nonlinear (hierarchical, parallel, web) to emphasize relationships. Three-dimensional layering shows layers atop one another: opaque layers for elaboration without switching context (pop-ups), transparent layers for overlays that combine to illustrate concepts (weather maps).
(c) For slides this means: a slide sequence is linear 2D layering (one layer per slide); overlays and progressive builds on a fixed base figure are transparent 3D layering that reveals relationships without losing context.

### Five Hat Racks
(a) There are five ways to organize information: category, time, location, alphabet, and continuum (magnitude).
(b) Category for clusters of similarity; time for chronological sequence and step-by-step procedures; location for spatial or geographic reference; alphabet for nonlinear reference lookup; continuum for ranking on a common measure. The choice of organization "dramatically influences which aspects of the information are emphasized."
(c) For slides this means: choose the ordering of results deliberately (by magnitude to argue a winner, by time to argue a trend, by category to argue coverage); the ordering itself is a rhetorical claim.

### Advance Organizer
(a) A brief chunk of information presented before new material, at a more abstract level than the material itself, so people understand new information in terms of what they already know.
(b) Expository organizers (define the big picture from scratch) when the audience has little or no relevant knowledge; comparative organizers (compare new to known) when the audience has similar existing knowledge. Works in linear presentations with a defined entry point; benefits are measurable though hard to validate. Distinct from an overview or summary, which merely lists key points.
(c) For slides this means: before the technical core, one abstract framing slide ("this decoder is like X you already know, except Y") tuned to whether the audience already knows X.

### Comparison
(a) Illustrate relationships and patterns by representing variables in controlled ways so comparisons are valid.
(b) Three techniques: apples to apples (common measures and units, corrected for confounds, same graphical and numerical standards); single context (patterns are harder to detect across separate pages or displays; prefer one multivariate display or small multiples in a single display); benchmarks (claims are meaningful only against reference values, like past performance, competitors, or standards).
(c) For slides this means: put competing methods on one slide with identical axes and units, as small multiples if needed, and always draw the baseline in the same panel as your result.

### Storytelling
(a) Creating imagery, emotion, and understanding of events through interaction between storyteller and audience; the original and most compelling method of passing knowledge.
(b) Fundamental elements that can rarely be subtracted without detriment: setting (orients in time and place), characters (identification makes it relevant), plot (ties events together), invisibility (the medium fades from awareness), mood (emotional tone), movement (clear, flowing sequence that does not stall). Audiences experience and recall a story personally.
(c) For slides this means: structure the talk as problem, struggle, resolution with the system or experiment as protagonist, and keep the deck mechanics invisible so nothing reminds the audience they are watching slides.

## 4. Perception

### Picture Superiority Effect
(a) Pictures are remembered better than words, and pictures plus words together beat either alone.
(b) The effect "applies only when people are asked to recall something after more than thirty seconds from the time of exposure"; immediate recall is equal for pictures and words. Strongest for common, concrete things versus abstract concepts, for distinct mixed pictures, and with casual, time-limited exposure; it diminishes as information grows more complex. Pictures and words must reinforce the same information: conflicting pairs "create interference and dramatically inhibit recall."
(c) For slides this means: pair every key claim with a concrete supporting image or diagram whose caption says the same thing; the audience will have forgotten your bullet text by the coffee break but not the picture.

### Iconic Representation
(a) Pictorial images make actions, objects, and concepts easier to find, recognize, learn, and remember, while saving space.
(b) Four types: similar icons (visual analogy, for simple concrete things), example icons (a typical associated object, for complex things, like an airplane meaning airport), symbolic icons (higher abstraction with well-established symbols, like a padlock), arbitrary icons (learned conventions, only for long-lived standards, like the radiation symbol). "Generally, icons should be labeled and share a common visual motif (style and color) for optimal performance."
(c) For slides this means: a consistent small icon language for recurring entities (qubit, decoder, GPU) speeds recognition across slides, but label the icons and keep one drawing style throughout.

### Color
(a) Color attracts attention, groups elements, indicates meaning, and enhances aesthetics; applied improperly it seriously harms form and function.
(b) The book's rules: "Use color conservatively. Limit the palette to what the eye can process at one glance (about five colors depending on the complexity of the design)." Never use color as the only carrier of information (a significant portion of the population has limited color vision). Aesthetic combinations: analogous, complementary, triadic, quadratic, or drawn from nature. "Use warmer colors for foreground elements, and cooler colors for background elements. Light gray is a safe color to use for grouping elements without competing with other colors." Saturation: saturated colors when attracting attention is the priority; desaturated when performance and efficiency are the priority; desaturated bright reads friendly and professional, desaturated dark reads serious and professional; combining saturated colors causes visual interference and eye fatigue. There is no substantive evidence for universal color-emotion effects or symbolism; meanings vary by culture, so verify with the audience.
(c) For slides this means: about five colors maximum across the whole deck, desaturated palette with one saturated accent for the thing that must pop, light gray for grouping and scaffolding, and every color distinction redundantly encoded (shape, label, position).

### Proximity
(a) Elements close together are perceived as more related than elements farther apart (Gestalt).
(b) Proximity "is one of the most powerful means of indicating relatedness in a design, and will generally overwhelm competing visual cues (e.g., similarity)." Arrange elements so proximity corresponds to relatedness; put labels and supporting information near what they describe, "opting for direct labeling on graphs over legends or keys"; keep unrelated items far apart. Touching or overlapping elements imply shared attributes; near but separate elements read as related but independent.
(c) For slides this means: label curves directly at the curve instead of using a legend, keep captions tight against their figures, and let whitespace gaps do the grouping that boxes would otherwise do.

### Similarity
(a) Similar elements are perceived as more related than dissimilar ones (Gestalt).
(b) "Similarity of color results in the strongest grouping effect; it is strongest when the number of colors is small, and is decreasingly effective as the number of colors increases." Size similarity works when sizes are clearly distinguishable; shape similarity "is the weakest grouping strategy," best when color and size are uniform or combined with them. Use the fewest colors and simplest shapes possible; because many viewers are color blind, pair color with another grouping cue.
(c) For slides this means: same color = same thing, everywhere in the deck; keep the color count low so the grouping stays strong, and back it with marker shape.

### Common Fate
(a) Elements that move (or flicker) together in the same direction are perceived as more related than elements moving differently or stationary (Gestalt).
(b) Relatedness is strongest with same time, velocity, and direction of motion (or same flicker time, frequency, intensity); dissimilar motion still groups if it forms a recognizable pattern or rhythm. Moving elements become figure, stationary ones ground.
(c) For slides this means: in animated builds, elements that appear or move together are read as one logical unit, so animate a label with its curve, and never animate unrelated items in the same step.

### Good Continuation
(a) Elements arranged on a straight line or smooth curve are perceived as a group and as more related than elements off the line (Gestalt).
(b) Locate elements so alignment corresponds to relatedness; unrelated items go on different alignment paths. Lines are perceived as continuing their established direction; hidden segments are bridged if extensions intersect with minimal disruption. Graph guidance: bars ordered so their tops form a continuous line are "more easily processed" than arrangements with abrupt, discontinuous tops.
(c) For slides this means: sort bar charts by value unless another ordering carries meaning, and route connector lines in diagrams smoothly, since sharp implausible bends break perceived connection.

### Uniform Connectedness
(a) Elements connected by uniform visual properties, such as a common region or a connecting line, are perceived as more related than unconnected elements (Gestalt).
(b) Two strategies: common regions (a bounding area groups everything inside) and connecting lines (an explicit line joins elements and can imply sequence). "Uniform connectedness will generally overpower the other Gestalt principles," beating proximity and similarity when they conflict, which makes it useful for correcting layouts that cannot be rearranged.
(c) For slides this means: a subtle background panel or a drawn connector is the strongest grouping tool you have; use it to bind a figure to its annotation, and to rescue groupings when layout constraints prevent moving things closer.

### Closure
(a) People perceive a set of individual elements as a single recognizable pattern, closing gaps and filling in missing information (Gestalt).
(b) Closure is strongest when elements approximate simple recognizable patterns and are near one another; "if the energy required to find or form a pattern is greater than the energy required to perceive the elements individually, closure will not occur." Removing completable lines reduces complexity and increases interest because viewers participate in completing the design; use transitional cues for complex patterns.
(c) For slides this means: you can delete plot borders, partial gridlines, and redundant outlines; viewers complete simple shapes themselves, and the sparser figure is both cleaner and more engaging.

### Aesthetic-Usability Effect
(a) Aesthetic designs are perceived as easier to use than less aesthetic designs, whether or not they actually are.
(b) First impressions bias long-term attitudes and resist change; aesthetic designs foster positive attitudes, make people "more tolerant of design problems," and promote creative thinking, while negative relationships narrow thinking. "Always aspire to create aesthetic designs."
(c) For slides this means: polish is not decoration; a visually careful deck buys audience patience for dense technical content and makes reviewers more forgiving of rough edges.

### 80/20 Rule
(a) Approximately 80 percent of the effects in any large system come from 20 percent of the variables (measured proportions vary between 10 percent and 30 percent).
(b) Examples given include "80 percent of a product's usage involves 20 percent of its features." Focus design and testing resources on the critical 20 percent; reevaluate the rest, and "noncritical functions that are part of the less-important 80 percent should be minimized or removed altogether." Optimizing beyond the critical 20 percent yields diminishing returns and can introduce new problems.
(c) For slides this means: identify the 20 percent of slides and figure elements that carry 80 percent of the argument, spend the polish budget there, and cut or back-shelve the rest.

### Recognition Over Recall
(a) Memory for recognizing things is better than memory for recalling them, because recognition tasks provide cues that narrow the memory search.
(b) Recognition memory develops from mere exposure and outlasts recall memory. "Minimize the need to recall information from memory whenever possible"; make options and referents clearly visible rather than requiring retrieval. Familiar options are chosen over unfamiliar ones, even when the unfamiliar one is objectively better.
(c) For slides this means: never require the audience to recall slide 7 while viewing slide 20; re-show the key figure, repeat the notation legend, and keep recurring visual anchors so everything needed is on screen.

## Resolving "too much white space" complaints

What the book's principles jointly imply when someone asks you to fill a sparse slide:

- The evidence runs the other way: Horror Vacui reports an inverse relationship between filled space and perceived value, with sophisticated audiences (a research audience is one) reading emptiness as prestige and clutter as cheapness. Filling space to look "complete" actively lowers perceived quality.
- Never resolve the complaint by adding elements. Signal-to-Noise Ratio and Ockham's Razor both say every added item steals attention from the result ("excess is noise"; remove elements, then minimize the expression of what remains). An added element that answers no question is pure noise.
- Resolve it by scaling up what is already there: enlarge the figure, the key number, and the type. Legibility supports this directly (larger type for low-resolution displays and distant viewers; contrast above 70 percent), and Figure-Ground says a bigger, higher-contrast figure element gets more attention and is better remembered. The slide reads fuller because the signal is bigger, not because there is more of it.
- Make remaining space look deliberate, not leftover: place the enlarged element on a thirds intersection or center it (Rule of Thirds), keep everything on a strict grid (Alignment, Area Alignment), and the empty area becomes composition instead of vacancy. Most "too empty" reactions are really "looks unbalanced or accidental" reactions.
- If more content is genuinely needed, add meaning, not marks: Propositional Density says to increase interpretations per element (a benchmark line, direct labels, a one-line takeaway under the figure), and Comparison says the single highest-value addition is a baseline in the same panel.
- Spread, don't stack: Progressive Disclosure and Chunking (4 plus or minus 1, not 7 plus or minus 2) say material that would crowd one slide should become two slides or a build; information shown before the audience needs it is effectively noise.
- Reserve density for lookup content: Chunking's own caveat says tables meant to be scanned should not be artificially sparsified, so a backup reference table may legitimately be dense while argument slides stay minimal.
- Keep one distinctive element per slide (von Restorff, Highlighting's 10 percent cap): a sparse slide with a single emphasized item is a memory device; a filled slide with several emphasized items is remembered as nothing in particular.
